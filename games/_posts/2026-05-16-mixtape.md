Mixtape is the high school nostalgia, saying goodbye to the friends you've had for your whole life, opening up the next chapter movie of video games.

I, perhaps unhealthily so, have the habit of growing nostalgic for things that are approaching their end, even as they're actively still happening.  And so, I pretty clearly remember how much I loved Can't Hardly Wait as an end-of-high-school movie even though it came out as I was still in high school. Mixtape is set in the exact same time period that I was also graduating from high school, and it really nails that moment perfectly for me.

In Mixtape, you're playing as Stacey Rockford on her last night in her small town with her friends Slater and Cassandra before taking off for New York City.  Stacey fancies herself able to pick the perfect song for any situation, and the game makes great use of this with its soundtrack.

Mixtape is structured as a series of locations that hold objects which bring back memories to the group.  As you investigate these objects, you'll eventually trigger a flashback which inevitably has Rockford introducing a song and explaining why it fits the moment.  Eventually you'll get to a mini-game to play for the flashback, and I found that this is where the combination of the game, the emotion of the moment in the flashback, and the music combined to make something just click.  I will not soon forget dashing through town playing effectively an auto-runner to John Paul Young's Yesterday's Hero.  Nor will I forget smashing home runs in softball as the stadium grows up around you and Stan Bush's The Touch plays.

Some of the mini-games are of course better than others, but none of them last for very long and all of them are used to strongly punctuate the moment in these teenager's lives that we're experiencing or remembering.

The softball game where you slowly have a full stadium with a jet fly-over appear around you highlights the surrealism that the game also plays in, where in truly emotional moments for each of the characters things start to get bigger than life.  Rockford will float away through the sky in sadness, or conduct a fireworks show with her hands in joy.  People will climb on top of school buses and clap her on to the beat of the song as she's rushing to help her friend.  Mixtape doesn't always deploy the surrealism trick, saving it for some of its more heightened moments, but when it does, it works incredibly well.

Are Rockford, Cassandra, and Slater a bit much?  Of course, but they're also seventeen, recent high school graduates looking to escape their small town, and are at that phase of their life where you naturally feel like you know everything and are above it all.  The characters' sometimes grating tendencies came off as authentic to the moment to me.

Playing a 1990s teenager on their last night with their friends had me not only thinking about my favorite coming of age movies, but also my strong memories of my own last summers, before leaving for college and before moving west for work.  Mixtape delivers on its premise by perfectly matching the music to gameplay moments, and uses its mini-games to really drive home the emotional beats in the story it's telling.
