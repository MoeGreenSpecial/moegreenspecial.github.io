Pragmata wasn't a game that I had been following much prior to its release, but it's one that I'm not soon going to forget now that I've played.  Its inventive combat system, fun gameplay, and top-notch visuals made me glad that I picked it up.

You're playing as Hugh, and are quickly joined by Diana, a pragmata who serves both as a 7-year-old who is eager to learn about Earth and also as your primary assistant in combat.  Together, you set out to discover what happened to all the people who were supposed to be working on the lunar outpost The Cradle.

And the combat is the real star of the show here.  Your enemies in Pragmata are all robots which have armor that is effectively impervious to your weapons.  Thankfully Diana knows how to hack the robots to disable their armor, allowing you to inflict damage.  Mechanically, you're using the face buttons to navigate around little puzzle grids on the right side of the screen in order to complete these hacks, all while maintaining your aim at the particular enemy you are hacking.

This split action between hacking with the face buttons and shooting with the usual trigger inputs was a bit mind-bending at first, but I quickly got the hang of it and it's an absolute blast.  The puzzle grids start out simple at first, just navigating to a goal square.  But they quickly evolve and become even more fun.  You'll wind up facing enemies that block off certain portions of the grid, until you manage to take down their shields.  You also run across upgrades which provide power-ups in the grid if you manage to work your way through their squares before hitting the goal square.  And, since those power ups are consumables, you wind up having to make decisions not only about which ones to equip but also about when the best time is to use them.  By the end of the game, I found myself focused more on setting up my grids and doing damage with them than I did on the gun play.  This is a really novel system, and it is by far my favorite part of the game.

In the world of Pragmata, the technology exists to 3D print nearly anything as long as you have enough Lim.  And thankfully the moon seems to be chock-full of the stuff, enabling The Cradle to be made up of an incredible variety of environments.  Pragmata takes advantage of this by structuring itself into five medium-sized levels that are all incredibly varied from one another.  You'll have a full cityscape in one level, while another one is all about nature, and a third is just about being out on the moon itself.  This combination of big, but not too big, levels along with the large variety between them really helps keep the game fresh as you move along through it.  Several of the levels also introduce new abilities to the game, which provides a light Metroidvania element.  These abilities typically can be used in previous levels to unlock areas that generally contain some collectibles.

Pragmata has a variety of collectibles in each level, and they all feel great to collect. There are various forms of upgrade currencies, ability unlocks, and most notably Read Earth Memories.  These REMs unlock portions of various Earth scenes in your hub area, which then opens up interactions between Hugh and Diana where you teach her about life on Earth.  These aren't just one-off scenes either, when you're hanging around the hub Diana will frequently be found playing with these REMs and the scenes they build.  It's a great combination of updating the cosmetics of your home base and also getting some meaningful character interaction between Hugh and Diana.

The Mini Cabin statues were another fun collectible type.  They are the only type of collectible that can't be scanned for, and instead make a sound when you get close enough to them.  You need to be constantly paying attention for the audio in order to hunt them all down.  Although this was the only collectible type that didn't provide an upgrade, the unique mechanics for finding them made the Mini Cabins really stand out.

The upgrades that you can buy for your abilities, weapons, and characters all feel meaningful.  After completing a level and spending my collected upgrade currency, I always felt much more mobile and powerful than I did before, and that in turn meant that hunting down the different upgrade currencies in the next level felt that much more rewarding.

There is an optional set of training missions you can do in your shelter which are all done without these upgrades.  As the game went on, doing those missions started to just feel bad because I was so used to having all of my upgraded attributes, playing without them didn't feel good.  Eventually, this caused me to give up on doing the training missions altogether because they simply weren't as fun as doing the main levels were.

Upgrading yourself and making good choices about your mods and loadout for each level meant that the game was challenging throughout while never really feeling punishing.  It felt rewarding to have made choices in your loadout that paid off in combat in each level, and the game generally felt fair the whole way through.  That is, until the massive difficulty spike at the final boss.  This was the only encounter that took me multiple attempts to get through with no clear path to improve my approach.  It felt a bit out of place compared to the combat in the rest of the game.

Pragmata is mostly a game about fun combat mechanics and the bonding between Hugh and Diana.  There's definitely some story here, but it comes into play mostly toward the end of the game.  While it was fine, and it does provide some good backstory for Diana as well as the explanation for what happened to The Cradle, it didn't pack the emotional punch that I think it was meant to.

But, in the end, the story pacing didn't really matter to me.  When I think back on Pragmata, the very first thing that's going to come to mind is the incredible puzzle combat mechanics and the developing bond between Hugh and Diana.  This game was incredibly fun to play, and that's what's going to stick with me for a long time.
